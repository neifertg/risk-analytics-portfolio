Part 1 · Why clustering fits audit work

Most audit populations have no label marking which record is the problem.

Supervised methodsClustering (unsupervised)
Need labeled examples: known fraud, known control failuresNo labels required
Learn to repeat a pattern you've already seenGroup records by similarity, then let the odd ones stand out
Only as good as your past examplesCan surface a pattern you've never seen before

Most audit testing starts from a population with no 'bad' column to learn from.

Delivery: no one hands the host a seating chart before the party starts, either — they read the room as guests actually arrive. This is the core motivation for using clustering in audit: fraud/error labels are rare, expensive, or don't exist yet for a new risk area. Clustering doesn't need them. Two things to set expectations on now, not later: the population still has to be reconciled to a control total before any flag is defensible as audit evidence (wiki: Data Quality for Analytics), and a clustering-based finding has to clear the same sufficient-and-appropriate-evidence bar as any other (wiki: IIA Standards). This isn't shadow IT — treat it that way from slide 1.

Part 2 · Three techniques, three audit questions

K-Means sorts records into a fixed number of segments so the odd segment stands out.

Nearest-centroid assignment, recomputed until stable — the host's 'nearest table,' reshuffled until it settles.

Delivery: this is the host's seating chart, formalized — decide the table count, seat everyone at their nearest table, keep reshuffling until it's stable. Audit use: segment vendors or transactions to find the outlier segment. Limit: requires choosing k upfront; assumes round, evenly sized groups. Source: wiki resources/k-means-clustering.md. Good fit: exploratory grouping of numeric data, e.g. customer/vendor segmentation. Poor fit: unknown k, non-round clusters, categorical-only data.

Part 2 · Three techniques, three audit questions

Hierarchical clustering builds a tree of groupings, so you decide the count last.

Repeatedly merges the two closest clusters — the party's circles, merging until you cut the tree.

Delivery: this is the party's conversation circles, formalized — small groups keep merging into bigger ones all night, and you only decide how many to call out after watching the whole thing play out. Audit use: explore population structure before setting a testing plan. Limit: O(n²) cost — doesn't scale to a full population; an early bad merge can't be undone. Source: wiki resources/hierarchical-clustering.md. Good fit: don't know k in advance, want to see nested structure, need deterministic results (no random init unlike K-Means). Poor fit: large datasets, noisy data with single linkage.

Part 2 · Three techniques, three audit questions

DBSCAN separates dense, ordinary activity from outliers automatically.

Dense regions become clusters automatically — the host's midnight lap, flagging whoever's standing alone by the punch bowl.

Delivery: this is the host's midnight lap of the room, formalized — dense knots of guests read as a group with no head count decided in advance, and whoever's standing alone by the punch bowl gets flagged instead of seated anywhere. Audit use: auto-flag transactions or log entries that fit no normal pattern. Limit: struggles with mixed-density populations; radius/threshold settings are fiddly. Source: wiki resources/dbscan.md. Good fit: arbitrary-shaped clusters, automatic outlier detection, unknown cluster count. Poor fit: high-dimensional data, mixed-density clusters.

Part 2 · Three techniques, three audit questions

K-Means

  • You choose the number of groups up front
  • Fast, scales well
  • Assumes round, evenly sized groups

Hierarchical

  • You choose the number of groups after seeing the tree
  • Deterministic, no random start
  • Doesn't scale to a full large population

DBSCAN

  • The technique finds the number of groups itself
  • Flags outliers automatically as noise
  • Struggles with mixed-density populations
Delivery: last call-back to the party before we get practical — the seating chart, the merging circles, the midnight lap, now with their real names side by side. Choosing among the three is mostly about whether you already know how many groups you're looking for, and whether automatic outlier-flagging matters more than speed. A fourth technique, Gaussian Mixture Models, gives each record a soft probability instead of a hard group — mention only if asked; it needs more data and tuning than a first pilot has, so it's deliberately not covered today (wiki: Gaussian Mixture Models). Whichever of these three you pick, expect real false-positive fatigue if the flagging rule isn't tuned — that's normal, not a sign the pilot failed (wiki: Continuous Auditing and Monitoring).

the memory and compute cost of hierarchical clustering — a real limit when testing a full transaction population rather than a sample.

Delivery: this is why the merging-circles approach falls apart at a stadium-sized party — comparing every guest to every other guest gets expensive fast, long before you'd ever finish merging. Part 3 · Applying it: business and IT audit examples

Clustering vendor payments can surface the one vendor that doesn't look like the rest.

Illustrative example — not a specific finding.

Delivery: same host's instinct, pointed at real vendor data instead of a room full of guests. Population: vendor payment records for a business cycle, tied out to a GL control total first (wiki: Data Quality for Analytics) — a flawless analysis of an unreconciled population is a confidently wrong one. Features grounded in known fraud red flags rather than generic behavior fields (wiki: Fraud Analytics and Red Flags): round-dollar concentration, just-under-threshold splitting, same requestor/approver pairs, weekend/period-end timing, vendor tenure/dormancy, and fuzzy-matched name or shared bank-account/tax-ID signals for ghost-vendor risk. This is a generic teaching example, not drawn from a real audit or organization. A flagged vendor is a lead to investigate, e.g. for duplicate payments or a shell-vendor risk — not a conclusion on its own.

Part 3 · Applying it: business and IT audit examples

Clustering access logs can flag the one account that doesn't match its peers.

Illustrative example — not a specific finding.

Delivery: the same instinct again, one more time on real data — the account that doesn't belong at its peer group's table. Population: access logs or privileged-account activity, IT general controls. Features: login times, systems accessed, transaction types, peer role. Complements the structural view in wiki: Segregation of Duties Analysis, which catches the conflict-matrix opportunity before it's exploited — this behavioral view catches unusual activity after the fact. This is a generic teaching example, not drawn from a real audit or organization. Peer-group definition (e.g. by role or department) matters a lot here — cluster within a peer group, not across the whole population. A flagged account is a lead, e.g. excessive access or a compromised credential — not a conclusion on its own.

Part 4 · Where to start

Vendor black-box

  • Packaged risk score — no analytics skill to run it
  • Fastest to a first result
  • Hardest to explain why a record was flagged

Point-and-click

  • IDEA, ACL/Diligent, Alteryx, Power BI — no code
  • Most departments' actual entry point
  • You can see and explain every step

Code

  • Python/scikit-learn — what this deck's techniques use
  • Most flexible and most transparent
  • Needs someone who's built that skill
Source: wiki resources/audit-analytics-tooling-landscape.md. This choice matters more to a first pilot's success than which algorithm from Part 2 you pick — a technically better algorithm nobody in the room can run loses to a worse one someone can actually operate. This usually isn't your call alone: it's a department licensing decision. What you can do today without waiting on that: prototype in whatever's already licensed to build the business case for more.

Part 4 · Where to start

A first clustering pilot, end to end

1. Pick a population
One with no existing 'bad' label, tied out to a control total first
2. Choose features
A handful of fields grounded in known fraud red flags, not identity
3. Run and review
K-Means or DBSCAN, in whatever tool you already have — expect mostly false positives
4. Fold into testing
Treat flagged records as leads for existing substantive procedures, not a final conclusion
Delivery: one last echo, then let it go — this is planning the party itself. This mirrors the make-deck render pipeline's own philosophy: start from the simplest version of each piece, and only add complexity (hierarchical, GMM) once the first pass earns it.
