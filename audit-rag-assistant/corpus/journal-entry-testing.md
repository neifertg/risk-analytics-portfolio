# Journal Entry Testing

## Why Journal Entries Get Targeted Testing

Manual journal entries bypass the automated controls built into
transaction-processing systems, which makes them the classic vehicle for
management override of controls — the risk that professional standards
specifically call out as present in every audit regardless of how strong
the rest of the control environment looks. Because of this, journal entry
testing is risk-targeted rather than randomly sampled.

## Risk Criteria for Selection

Pull the full population of manual journal entries for the period and
flag entries matching risk criteria: postings made late at night or on
weekends, entries posted and approved by the same user, round-dollar
entries, entries to accounts with a history of manipulation (reserves,
accruals, unusual account pairings), and entries made in the days
immediately before period-end close. An entry matching several criteria
at once is higher risk than one matching a single criterion, so score
entries rather than treating every flag as equally significant.

## Testing Selected Entries

For each selected entry, obtain supporting documentation for the business
rationale, confirm the account coding is appropriate for the transaction
described, and verify the approval — if the system allows a preparer to
also approve their own entry, that itself is a control gap worth noting
independent of whether any individual entry looks improper. An entry with
no supporting documentation beyond "correcting entry" is a finding
regardless of whether the correction turns out to be legitimate.

## Aggregating Findings

Individual journal entry exceptions should be aggregated and evaluated
together, not just closed one at a time — a pattern of similar entries
from the same preparer, or a cluster of adjustments to the same account
across multiple periods, can indicate a systemic issue that no single
entry would reveal on its own.
